Every new cohort of fellows and interns asks the same question in their first week: what should I read? There is no single answer, but here are five books that have repaid the time of many people starting out. They disagree with each other in useful ways.

**1. Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen, *An Uncertain Glory: India and Its Contradictions* (2013).** A sustained argument that India's growth has not been matched by progress in health, education and public services, with frequent reference to Kerala's experience.

**2. Devesh Kapur, Pratap Bhanu Mehta and Milan Vaishnav (eds.), *Rethinking Public Institutions in India* (2017).** Essays on the institutions that actually run the country, from the Election Commission to the civil services. Essential for understanding why good policies often fail in implementation.

**3. Robin Jeffrey, *Politics, Women and Well-Being: How Kerala Became 'a Model'* (1992).** A historian's account of how social movements, politics and the position of women shaped Kerala's distinctive development.

**4. Abhijit V. Banerjee and Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics* (2011).** A readable introduction to evidence based thinking about poverty, and to the careful testing of policy ideas.

**5. Y. V. Reddy and G. R. Reddy, *Indian Fiscal Federalism* (2019).** A clear guide to how money flows between the Union and the states, by authors with long experience of the system.

Read them with a pencil. Note where you disagree, and why. The habit of arguing with what you read is the most useful thing a reading list can teach.
