The Vizhinjam International Seaport has been discussed for so long that its arrival can feel like the end of a story. It is the beginning of one.

Transhipment ports move containers from very large ships to smaller ones. Much of that cargo never leaves the port. A port of this kind can be highly automated, which means that the number of jobs inside the gate is modest. The larger economic effect comes from what develops around it: logistics parks, ship services, warehousing, repair, finance, hospitality, and the housing and services that the people working in them need.

None of that happens automatically. Ports elsewhere in the world have become engines of regional growth where land, transport links and skills were planned in advance. They have become enclaves, surrounded by congestion and resentment, where they were not.

Three things should be at the top of the agenda.

**First, a plan for the land around the port.** Without one, speculation and unplanned building will make good development harder and more expensive. The plan should protect fishing villages and their access to the sea.

**Second, connections that work.** Road and rail links to the port must be designed to keep heavy freight out of residential streets and away from the city's already crowded roads.

**Third, skills for local people.** Training programs for port, logistics and maritime jobs should be designed with employers now, with places reserved for young people from the coastal communities most affected by the port.

The port is a large public investment. Its return will be measured not in containers handled, but in what it does for the people of Thiruvananthapuram and the coast.
